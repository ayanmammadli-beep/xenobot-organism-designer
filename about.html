Overview

The AI Scientist for Xenobots

MorphoCapture turns your research ideas into living machines. Simply define a functional objective — like cleaning an oil spill, unclogging an artery, or delivering a drug — and our AI platform generates optimized xenobot designs and step-by-step lab protocols to test your organism.

Feed your experimental results back to the AI scientist to refine future designs, making your xenobots smarter, more efficient, and ready for real-world deployment.

Biology is the next great engineering platform — and we're building the infrastructure to program it.

Background

Scientific Foundation

In 2020, researchers at Tufts University and the University of Vermont discovered that stem cells from Xenopus laevis embryos can spontaneously self-organize into novel multicellular structures called xenobots. These tiny living machines exhibit locomotion, collective behavior, and self-repair — all without genetic modification.

Design Methodology

The original design process used evolutionary algorithms on supercomputers to simulate thousands of cellular arrangements. Designs were evaluated for specific tasks, such as moving or manipulating objects. The most promising structures were then assembled by hand using precise micromanipulation techniques.

Key Findings

In 2021, researchers showed xenobots could reproduce by gathering loose cells and forming new functional structures. This was the first demonstration of AI-designed biological systems capable of self-replication, confirming the potential of programmable living machines.

Applications

Targeted Medicine

Delivering drugs to specific tissues or organs, clearing arterial plaque, or assisting in precision therapies where conventional devices cannot reach.

Synthetic Biology & Bioengineering

Acting as programmable living tools for tissue patterning, organoid assembly, or experimental manipulation of multicellular systems.

Industrial / Micromanufacturing

Manipulating small particles, self-assembling materials, or performing tasks in microfluidic or otherwise inaccessible environments.

Problem Statement

Current Limitations

Xenobots show incredible potential, but designing and building them is slow, complex, and confined to a few specialized labs. Creating new designs requires large-scale simulations and careful cell assembly under a microscope — a process that can take weeks and demands both computational and wet-lab expertise.

Most research institutions lack the tools, infrastructure, or cross-disciplinary knowledge needed, creating a major bottleneck in turning xenobot science into real-world solutions.

Platform

MorphoCapture Workflow: Microplastic Collection

Step 1
Define Objective & Set Parameters
Researcher sets the goal: collect microplastics from a water environment. Define parameters such as fluid viscosity, temperature, particle size, and flow conditions.
⬇

Step 2: AI Design & Simulation

Genome Encoding (CPPNs)

Each candidate xenobot design starts as a Compositional Pattern Producing Network (CPPN). The CPPN takes spatial coordinates (x, y, z) as input and outputs the cell type for each voxel, creating structured, symmetric, and biologically plausible morphologies.

Physics Simulation

The candidate morphology is simulated in a voxel-based soft-body simulator called Voxcraft, placed in a parameterized fluid environment that mirrors real-world conditions. The xenobot moves, contracts, and interacts with its surroundings, such as microplastic particles.

Fitness Evaluation

After the simulation, the system calculates a fitness score based on the task:

Movement Efficiency:
Fmovement = dCOM / T
Microplastic Collection:
Fmicroplastic = w1(Ncollected / Ntotal) + w2(dCOM / T) + w3(Sstability / Smax)
Where:
Ncollected = number of microplastic particles gathered
Ntotal = total particles in the environment
dCOM / T = movement efficiency
Sstability / Smax = structural integrity of the xenobot
w1, w2, w3 = weighting factors prioritizing collection, movement, and stability

Evolutionary Algorithm

The population of CPPNs is ranked by fitness, and the best-performing candidates are evolved through tournament selection, mutation, and crossover. This iterative process continues until the platform identifies the optimal xenobot design for the specified objective, ready for lab assembly and testing.

⬇
Step 3
Protocol Generation
The platform produces step-by-step instructions for assembling the winning xenobot in the lab.
⬇
Step 4
Experimental Testing
Researchers build the xenobots and test them in water tanks with microplastics. Data on collection efficiency, speed, and durability is recorded.
⬇
Step 5
Feedback Loop

⬇
Step 6
Deployment & Iteration
Optimized xenobots are ready for scaled-up trials. Continuous iteration enhances efficiency, reliability, and adaptability to varying water conditions.

Appendix

Physics & Real-World Considerations

To ensure xenobots designed in MorphoCapture perform in real-world water environments, our simulations account for key physical forces and constraints affecting movement, particle interactions, and durability:

1. Brownian Motion
Thermal Diffusion
Random thermal diffusion of particles, influenced by temperature, particle size, and fluid viscosity:
D = kBT / (6πηr)
Where:
D = diffusion coefficient
kB = Boltzmann's constant
T = absolute temperature
η = fluid viscosity
r = particle radius
2. Stokes Drag
Fluid Resistance
Resistance experienced by the xenobot or particles moving through fluid:
Fdrag = 6πηrv
Where:
Fdrag = drag force
v = particle or xenobot velocity
3. Directional Flow
Bulk Fluid Currents
Bulk fluid currents add velocity vectors to particles and xenobots:
vtotal = vbot + vflow
Where:
vbot = xenobot's velocity from contraction
vflow = environmental fluid flow velocity
4. Electrostatic Adhesion
Particle Attachment
Determines particle attachment to xenobot surfaces:
Felec ∝ (q1q2) / (εr2) · e−κr
Where:
q1, q2 = surface charges on particle and xenobot
ε = permittivity of the medium
r = separation distance
κ = Debye-Hückel screening constant (depends on salinity/pH)
5. Soft-Body Contraction
Movement Mechanics
Contractile cells generate movement via asymmetric contraction of voxel-spring networks:
Fcontractile = k(L − L0(t))
Where:
k = spring constant connecting voxels
L = current voxel distance
L0(t) = time-dependent rest length of contractile cells
6. Environmental Factors
Real-World Conditions
Temperature, pH, salinity, obstacles, and debris modify diffusion, adhesion, and contraction parameters to ensure simulations match realistic deployment conditions.

Optimization Models

NEAT Evolution

Evolves CPPN-based xenobot morphologies over generations, selecting designs that perform best on the fitness function.

"NEAT proposes new body plans by iteratively mutating and combining high-performing designs."

Bayesian Optimization

Guides the search toward the most promising candidate morphologies without simulating every possibility.

"Bayesian optimization predicts which designs are likely to succeed, saving simulation time and resources."

Gradient Ascent

Fine-tunes design parameters to incrementally improve fitness within a candidate morphology.

"Gradient ascent adjusts cell types and properties to maximize performance in specific tasks."

By modeling these forces mathematically, MorphoCapture ensures that fitness scores and AI-optimized xenobot designs translate into reliable real-world performance, improving both lab experiments and field deployment outcomes.
